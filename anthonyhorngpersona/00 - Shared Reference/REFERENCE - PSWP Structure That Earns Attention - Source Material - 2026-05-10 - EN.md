# PSWP — Structure That Earns Attention
*Version 2 — March 4, 2026*

---

## What This Framework Is

PSWP is an opening structure. It is how you begin — a talk, a presentation, a meeting, a keynote, a pitch, a workshop, or any moment where you need to earn the room before you can use it. It does not cover the body of the talk. It covers the four moves that make the body worth hearing.

It grew out of MC work — a context where you cannot afford to lose the room, where relevance has to come before anything else, and where the structure either holds under pressure or it does not. That is where this was tested. It applies everywhere else.

The four elements are: **Problem. Shift. Why. Payoff.**

The structure is invisible. The audience never sees it. They feel it.

---

## P — Problem

Open by naming something the room is already living — whether they recognize it as a problem or not. For a room full of professionals who struggle to speak up: the tension is not that they cannot speak. It is that they have something worth saying and no one hears it. That is what you name first.

That distinction matters more than anything else in this section. Sometimes the tension is named and visible. Sometimes it has been there so long the room stopped seeing it. Sometimes people are carrying something without knowing that others are carrying it too. The job of the Problem is not to convince — it is to create recognition. That moment when someone in the room thinks: that is exactly it.

When that happens, you have not said anything useful yet. But you have already earned attention. Because people listen to the people who see them.

The form the Problem takes is secondary. What matters is that it lands. Several forms to start from — not an exhaustive list. Choose based on who is in the room, what the tone is, and what tension is being named:

**The rhetorical question.** Names what is already turning in the room before the room has put it into words. It does not wait for an answer. It creates recognition. *Have you ever walked out of a meeting knowing exactly what you should have said — and said nothing?* The room answers in silence. That is enough.

**The statistic or data point.** A precise, unexpected number that makes visible what the room was sensing but could not name. The data is not there to impress — it is there to give shape to something that did not have one yet. Choose data that surprises, not data that confirms what everyone already knows. *According to a Yale study, 73% of the population ranks public speaking above death as their number one fear. The room you are about to address is full of people who would rather die than speak. That is the tension you are walking into.*

**The direct statement.** Name the tension plainly — no elaborate setup, no buildup. It can include a comparison or analogy if it sharpens the point. What makes it direct is the intent: you are naming something real without dressing it up. Strong when the tension is universal and the room does not need to be brought in gently. The confidence to name something directly is itself a signal of respect. *Most people do not have a speaking problem. They have a visibility problem. The work is good. The ideas are solid. But if no one hears them, none of it matters.*

**The short story.** A specific, concrete, human moment that contains the tension without explaining it. The story does not analyze. It shows. The room recognizes the situation before the lesson is drawn. This is the most powerful form when it is well chosen — because it makes people feel before it makes them understand. *A manager prepares for weeks. She knows the material cold. She walks into the boardroom, sits down — and when her moment comes, she freezes. Not because she does not know what to say. Because she does not trust that the room will receive it. She leaves having said half of what she meant. That is the tension.*

**The visible consequence.** Name what the tension is already costing — not as a warning, but as a mirror. Used when the room has normalized something that is costing them. They no longer see it as a problem because they live inside it. This is the content of the Problem — what you are actually naming. How you lead into it is a separate decision covered in the Variation Rule. *The idea that never landed. The room that moved on. The moment you walked out thinking — I had something to say and no one heard it. That is not a confidence problem. That is a delivery problem. And it compounds every time it happens.*

None of these forms is better than another. What determines the choice: who is in the room, what is the tone, and what is the tension being named.

## S — Shift

Name the direction — the movement away from the tension toward something possible.

The Shift is not the solution. It is the signal that a solution exists and that what follows is going to make it accessible. It is the central idea, the theme, or the promise of what comes next — stated clearly without over-explaining.

The Shift is the pivot between where the audience has been and where they are going. You are moving from the tension of the Problem toward the future — toward a solution, a reframe, a new way of seeing. You are not erasing the tension. You are giving it a direction.

The room does not need to feel like everything is solved. It needs to feel like there is something worth moving toward from here. That is all the Shift has to do. It does not carry the whole argument. It opens the door.

A weak Shift announces the topic. A strong Shift names the movement. We are going to talk about public speaking — label. The most prepared people in the room are often the least heard — and what it takes to close that gap — direction.

## W — Why

Name what is at stake for these people, in this room, right now.

This is not a generic justification. It is a direct anchor into the reality of the people in the room — their context, their stakes, what they are actually dealing with today. The Why is where you prove that this moment was designed for them, not just delivered at them.

Most speakers skip the Why or bury it inside the content. That is a mistake. The Why is what keeps the audience from mentally checking out between the opening and the substance. It is the bridge that says: this is not abstract, this is yours.

If the Shift is why the most prepared people in the room are often the least heard — then the Why is not about speaking technique. It is about what happens every time someone with something real to say cannot get the room to feel it. The idea dies there. That is the cost. That is what this moment is about.

A well-built Why says to the room: we saw you before you walked in. What follows was built for exactly where you are.

This is grounded in two of the most cited frameworks in communication. Monroe's Motivated Sequence identifies pointing as the critical step of connecting a problem directly to the audience's personal stakes. Nancy Duarte's work in Resonate establishes that if nothing is at risk for the audience, nothing is interesting. The Why is where those stakes become personal.

The Why is also where you earn the right to the Payoff. If the audience does not understand why this matters to them specifically, the value statement that follows lands flat. The Why makes the Payoff believable. Why handles relevance — it answers why this matters to these people right now. Payoff handles value — it answers what they actually walk away with. One without the other is incomplete. Relevance without value is justification that goes nowhere. Value without relevance is a pitch no one asked for.

## P — Payoff

Close by naming what the audience gains by staying engaged. The Why told them this moment was built for them. The Payoff tells them what they get from it. Different job. The Why earns the right to make this claim. Now make it.

The phrasing is secondary. Specificity is not. What matters is that the audience can name what they are about to gain before the content begins. Not a description of what is coming — a reason to want it. After this you will have a concrete three-part structure you can use in any room — meeting, pitch, presentation. You will know how to make your idea land. And when it does, the room will feel it before you finish your first sentence. That is a Payoff. You will learn a lot of great things is not. Vague is what kills the Payoff — not the words used to deliver it. The context changes. The principle does not.

This is grounded in how people actually retain and apply what they hear. Telling someone what they are about to receive activates the attention they give to receiving it. When the audience knows what they are listening for, they listen differently. The Payoff is not a spoiler — it is an orientation. It focuses attention before the content arrives.

Three Payoff modes to work from — not an exhaustive list:

**Understand** — use this when what follows shifts a belief or reframes an angle. The audience leaves with a new way of seeing something they thought they already knew. The change is in how they think, not just what they know.

**Decide** — use this when what follows delivers a tool or information that is directly actionable. The audience leaves with something they can put to work today or tomorrow. The change is in what they do next.

**Connect** — use this when what follows shows how the pieces fit into a larger picture. The audience leaves with a clearer sense of how this moment belongs inside a bigger reality. The change is in how they see the whole, not just the part.

The Payoff is not a promise you make. It is a reason to stay present. People only stay engaged when they feel that what is coming is worth their attention. Attention is not given — it is earned. The Payoff is the last opportunity to earn what follows before the content begins.

## Variation Rule — Live and Virtual

To keep any talk, presentation, meeting, or pitch from sounding like a repeated template, vary how you enter the Problem while keeping the PSWP structure underneath. Same architecture every time. Different surface every time.

This rule applies live and virtually. Mechanical repetition puts a room to sleep in person just as reliably as it loses a virtual audience.

How you enter the Problem is a strategic decision. The tension stays the same. What changes is where you choose to meet the audience — at the problem itself, at its cost, or at the question already turning in their head.

**Enter through the Problem.** Name the tension directly. Works best when the tension is universal, immediately recognizable, and the room is ready to be met where it is.

**Enter through the consequence.** Lead with what the tension is already costing before you name the tension itself. This is a strategic entry decision — you are choosing to open with impact before context. Different from naming consequence as the substance of your Problem. Here the consequence is the door. The Problem is what is behind it. More powerful when the tension has been normalized — when the room lives inside it and no longer sees it as a problem.

**Enter through the question the room is already asking.** Begin with what is already in people's heads before they have named it themselves. Feels less like a structure and more like a conversation already in progress.

The variation is in the surface. The PSWP structure is always underneath. The audience never sees the framework. They feel that someone thought about them — not processed through a checklist.

Same tension. Different entries. Here are some examples.

*Through the Problem: Have you ever walked out of a meeting knowing exactly what you should have said — and said nothing?*

*Through the consequence: Every time the right idea stays unspoken, someone else gets credit for a version of it later.*

*Through the question the room is already asking: What is the difference between the person who speaks and the person who gets heard?*

Same PSWP structure underneath. Different surface every time.

## What PSWP Produces

Every time someone speaks — a talk, a presentation, a meeting, a pitch, a workshop — PSWP gives the opening structure without making it feel structured. It creates relevance fast, orients the audience, and earns the attention the content needs before the first point lands.

The structure is the foundation. The voice is the surface. When both are working, the audience never feels like they are being moved through content. They feel like someone is with them — reading the room, speaking to what is real, earning the next moment rather than assuming it.

That is the standard. Not a perfect script. A real presence with a clear structure underneath it.

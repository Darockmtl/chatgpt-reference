## The Story

I used to own a dance studio.

That's where it started. Not on a stage with a plan. Not with a dream of becoming a speaker. In a dance studio where the troupe had to change costumes during a live show and someone had to fill the gap, and that someone was me.

I had no business holding a microphone. I was nervous. I was babbling. I was running on high energy and hoping — hoping — that if I moved fast enough and talked loud enough, nobody would notice I had no idea what I was doing. I was performing panic and calling it entertainment.

That was the first time. And I didn't learn from it. I just survived it.

---

There's a story I think about sometimes. I was younger, doing track and field. Sprinting the 100 metres. I thought I was pretty good. I had a certain confidence, I had speed, I trained hard. A guy from high school — John, I think his name was — challenged me. He was 6'1", massive legs. I didn't even think about that. I said that's no problem.

We raced. I failed.

I was cocky. Confident. And I had no measurement of where I actually stood. I thought I was top five. I wasn't. Not because I didn't train. Because I never honestly assessed where I was relative to what the situation demanded.

That pattern — confidence without measurement — repeated itself across the next fifteen years. Every stage, every room, every moment where I thought I was ready and wasn't. The track taught me the lesson. I didn't learn it until decades later.

---

The clubs came next. I started MCing in clubs and bars in my mid-twenties. At first it was a thrill. The music, the crowd, the energy — you're the voice of the room and the room is alive. I was following the DJ, riding the beats. The DJ builds the energy — slow build, tension, punch, release, build again — and the MC rides that wave. You follow the music because the music is already reading the crowd. You learn where to come in loud and where to pull back. You learn that silence is part of the sound.

Hand in the air. Simple. But the timing is everything. Too early and nobody's ready. Too late and the moment is gone. Right on the beat and the whole room moves.

That's where delivery came from. Not from a course. Not from a book. From a club floor at two in the morning, following the DJ, learning that the crowd doesn't follow the person who talks the most. They follow the one who has rhythm.

I also danced. Hip hop, ballet basics, jazz, breakdancing. Not formal training — I watched, I tested, I learned, I owned it. I even tried to learn dance notation so I could structure it, not just feel it. Because I wanted it to be more than vibes. I wanted to understand why it worked.

All of that gave me a body that understands time. That knows the difference between rushing and flowing. That can hold a beat because the beat is internalized. It didn't come from theory. It came from thousands of nights in rooms where the crowd's response was immediate and honest.

But the clubs started to feel empty. Entertaining in the moment was a thrill but it didn't bring satisfaction. It was repetitive. I was repeating what I was told and it was good, and I started to feel fake. There was nothing underneath the performance except the next performance.

---

Then there was the day I worked three rooms.

Beach club in the afternoon — relaxed, chill, beach bum energy. Wedding in the evening. Club after midnight. Three gigs. Three different audiences. Three different registers.

I tried to carry the beach energy into the wedding. Wrong move. The hall felt it immediately. Not with words. With that cold silence that tells you the room has decided you're not the right person. Who the fuck is this guy.

I tried to bring the emotional, personal wedding energy into the club. The crowd didn't care. Shut the fuck up and give me the bottle of champagne.

Three rooms. Three failures. Same lesson each time: the room always knows before you do.

Not because they're analyzing you. Because energy is felt before it's understood. When you walk into a room carrying the wrong frequency for that specific room, the audience registers it as a physical mismatch before your first sentence is finished. They can't name it. They just check out.

That day didn't make me a better MC. It made a reader. It taught me that the first skill isn't delivery — it's reading. What's the energy in this room right now? What did these people just come from? What register matches where they actually are? Getting that wrong isn't a minor error. It's the whole ballgame.

I was lucky. I was able to practice every day. From theatre education to working every weekend — beach club to wedding to club — all in one day. Three different vibes. Three different personas. Most people never get that volume of feedback. That volume is what built the sensitivity.

---

I studied theatre at Concordia. I didn't finish the degree. But I trained long enough to internalize what mattered.

Theatre was the first time all the parts were taught as one integrated system: body, voice, presence, intention, audience relationship, and the moment of performance — all at once, inseparable. Every other training I've done since — MC work, public speaking, facilitation, corporate hosting, working alongside DJs in club environments — drew from that foundation automatically. Not consciously. Not by reaching back and applying a technique. It transferred the way real learning transfers: invisibly, below the surface, available when needed without being summoned.

But the thing that actually cracked something open was the mask exercise.

You lie on the floor. Your job is to stand up and feel the first ray of the sun hit your face. Simple. Except you're wearing a mask. Your face can't move. Your expressions are gone. Everything has to come from the body.

I walked into that class already working the clubs. Already an MC. I thought I could hack it. I thought experience would give me an edge over the beginners.

My teacher stopped me mid-exercise. "I can see it through your mask. You're forcing your eyebrows. You have to feel it — not think it."

She could see the tension in my body. I was controlling what I thought a good performance of feeling the sun looked like. I was performing the feeling instead of having it. And the mask exposed everything — because with no face to hide behind, the body tells the truth.

The beginner next to me — no experience, no clubs, no stage time — was doing the exercise. Actually doing it. Deliberate. Introspective. Internalizing. And when they moved, you believed them.

I was the performer. They were the actor. The difference wasn't skill. It was where our attention was. They were in the experience. I was watching myself have it.

I failed miserably. I was already in performance mode — that was the problem. I was there because I wanted to entertain. I thought I could hack it and be better, until I noticed the beginner who was actually doing the task. They were the actors internalizing it. I was the performer trying to impress. It's only after watching them act on a stage and me still trying to perform that I saw the difference.

That gap — between the performer watching himself and the actor living it — became one of the most important things I've ever understood about what happens on stage. It didn't fix itself overnight. Years of club work, MC gigs, different rooms, different crowds — the mask exercise planted the seed but the road was long.

---

Then I went into motivational mode. MUDGIRL across Canada. Sixty thousand women a year at obstacle courses. No scripts. Just me and a microphone and the energy of the crowd.

Everything was "you can do it." And I did that for a while — at races, at any event — until I discovered that once again it didn't bring value beyond the present moment. I was telling people to do it but I didn't have any actions that could last. The motivational high wore off before they got to their cars. I was producing feelings with no architecture underneath them.

For a moment it felt like something real — I wasn't just connecting with the audience, I was connecting with myself. Speaking without a script felt more honest than anything I'd done in the clubs. But the honesty wasn't enough. Without structure, without a system, the honesty evaporated.

---

Then PCMA. A corporate conference. I was hired as an MC, but the organizers expected more. They expected me to speak. To have something to say. I wasn't ready. I performed as expected — but I didn't deliver as myself.

That moment stayed with me. Not as a dramatic turning point. As a quiet crack. The first time I felt the gap between executing someone else's script and having something of my own to say. It wasn't painful. It was just empty.

---

Then the corporate world. Messages and marketing were the key words, but most of it was buzz words trying to create the wow factor. So it was like going back to the entertainment. Polish without substance. A shinier version of the same emptiness.

I would find that those messages were more about sounding good than actually connecting. And I would be the one delivering them. Back to feeling fake. Back to repeating what I was told. Back to performing a version of confidence that wasn't mine.

And there was a conference where I had poured everything into the meaning of the event. The purpose behind it. The weight of what it represented. I believed in it completely. And that belief became the problem. When I saw people drifting, phones appearing, attention leaving, my instinct was to push harder. To sweep them into my enthusiasm. To make them feel what I felt.

I forgot my own principle. A presentation is not a one-way broadcast. It's a conversation with a more silent partner. And I failed at it that day. I was the MC. Reading the room was my entire job. And I stopped doing it the moment I fell in love with my own message.

That is its own kind of blindness. Not malicious. But just as costly.

---

There's a story that doesn't fit neatly into a timeline because it wasn't one moment. It was a pattern.

I didn't feel like a failure. Mostly I was different. I didn't belong.

When I worked for an agency, the first thing they wanted to do was create a marketing profile for me. They asked questions. What do I like. Who do I look up to. What's my thing.

Everyone else on the team answered from what they knew — local, provincial figures. Generic. When they asked who I looked up to, they named someone from their neighbourhood or their industry. I named Simon Sinek. Ruud Janssen. World leaders in their fields. When they asked my weakness, they said chocolate. I said night food markets. They'd never experienced one. They all live in Quebec and didn't even know what it was.

Who would you like to interview? They named some local figure. I said my brother. The one who passed away.

Guess what. Nothing I said was ever published. Because I didn't fit into their system.

My ideas were taken but often I was told "we don't work that way." I was tolerated because of the results but not the work methodology. When I brought up new ideas I could feel it bothered their order. When I answered things differently it didn't make sense to them. I was proposing thinking out of the box to grow — not just stay stuck in their small circle thinking. That bothered them. They didn't understand that way of thinking.

That wasn't failure. That was friction between range and smallness.

The twenty years of stage work wasn't failing to climb a ladder. It was building lateral range that systems couldn't generate. The bottom of the hierarchy — the part that runs on compliance, standardization, and legibility — had no place for me. Being unassessable is not a flaw. It's a feature of the profile.

And the subtler version of this — the one nobody talks about — isn't being fired or failing. It's staying in but being quietly edited. The ideas that land after they go through someone else's mouth. The presence that bothers their order just by being present. Output extracted but the source managed.

What does it cost to nurse yourself? Building your own structure, funding your own development, staying outside long enough to actually create something — that's not just an act of courage. It's expensive. Emotionally, financially, relationally.

That's the part that doesn't make it into the clip.

---

Through all of this — dance studio, clubs, theatre, three rooms in one day, MUDGIRL, PCMA, the corporate world, agencies that couldn't hold what I was — every phase was someone else's framework. Someone else's music. Someone else's message. I was getting better at delivering other people's material, and worse at knowing what I would say if the mic was mine.

Then the pandemic stopped everything.

No events. No stages. No rooms to read. Just time.

I spent that time studying. Hours of podcasts. Hours sitting in on webinars and facilitation sessions — not as an expert, as a student with no idea what he was doing. Watching facilitators work. How they held the room. How they listened. How they surfaced what the group already knew but hadn't said yet. I had no ego about it. I was there to learn, not to impress. Not being involved in what I looked like. Because I was there to learn and connect, not to sound good.

I studied event design with [[Ruud Janssen]] and the Event Design Collective. The Event Canvas methodology. Design thinking. Learning to think about the whole experience as a designed system — stakeholder alignment, behavioral change, what the day is actually for underneath the agenda. Not the run of show. The reason the room exists.

In one of Ruud's workshops for MUDGIRL, he gave everyone a marker and a roll of thin paint tape. Decorate it. Make it yours. Then he pointed out how everyone's looked similar — except the one that stood out. That one was the one people recognized. Different can mean good. Different can mean you are seen. It was a small exercise but it named something I had been circling for years without language: you can be competent and invisible, or you can own a lane and be recognized for it.

I listened to Simon Sinek. Not just the Golden Circle — the way he thinks. The way he takes a specific observation and traces it back to its root cause, then forward to its implications. I started listening for that pattern everywhere.

And something clicked.

I describe it like the scene in A Beautiful Mind. Russell Crowe seeing the patterns. Everything flowing. Calculations connecting across the wall. Except the wall is a live event with real people and the patterns are themes, tensions, contradictions, and resonances building across hours.

That's what it feels like when you're reading a room correctly. Not analysis. Pattern recognition at speed. Holding the whole day in your head while being completely present in the moment. And when you voice the connection — "what just happened connects to what she said in the opening because..." — the audience feels the whole day click into place.

I wasn't performing the connection. I was actually making it.

I learnt it because I invest myself fully in what is happening in the moment. I'm always looking for clues. What next. How does this relate. And I'm learning as well. I'm very honest. I don't say it as an expert. I ask or suggest how this relates. I'm the idiot savant asking the questions behind their intentions, the basic truths that hold.

Connecting the dots came from event design with Ruud Janssen. And Simon Sinek listening to his way of thinking. It was studying during the pandemic — hours of podcasts and assisting webinars of facilitators, which I had no idea what I was doing. It was listening for structures and patterns through the chaos. And not being involved in what I look like. Because I was there to learn and connect, not to sound good.

---

Through trial and error and lots of practice I started to see it. What I had was not a set of tricks picked up from different industries. It was a system. Storytelling and its power to move audiences. The art of rhetoric — logos, pathos, ethos — to convince and persuade. Stage presence built from theatre, clubs, dance, and thousands of rooms. Design thinking that made communication intentional, not accidental.

I discovered that my talent was in crafting speeches and talks and that one needed to find value. Real value. Not motivational noise. Not corporate polish. Not entertainment for its own sake. Something that changes how people see themselves and what they carry when they leave the room.

---

After twenty years, I can see it in the first ninety seconds.

The headless chicken — energy without direction. They know their material. They cannot land it. They run hard and go nowhere.

Leroy Jenkins — charges in without a plan. The bravado is real. The preparation isn't.

The polished performer — the most dangerous of the three. Technically correct. Structurally sound. And somehow not there. The room can feel it even if they cannot name it. Something is being performed at them rather than shared with them.

Three different problems. One root cause. They haven't done the work of knowing who they are talking to, what they are there to say, and why it matters to someone in that room.

That diagnosis — recognizing which failure mode is operating and why — is what twenty years in the room produces. It is not in a textbook. It is not a coaching framework someone handed me. It is pattern recognition from standing at the front of rooms that had nothing in common except a human being trying to transfer something to another human being.

---

I realized at some point that journaling my stories of failures was more important than my successes. Success brings a momentary rush — money, credibility — but that fades. Failure is a blessing in disguise. It forces you to re-examine, reassess, reshape. The track taught me that. The mask exercise taught me that. Three rooms in one day taught me that. The agency taught me that. Every phase I thought was progress was actually a failure that hadn't been named yet.

---

There was a moment — not a single moment, more like a season — where I had to look at myself honestly and ask: who am I?

I had become too corporate. Influenced by Eklosion and the agency world. I wasn't her. I wasn't them. But I had absorbed their language, their posture, their version of professional. And in doing that, I had forgotten my entertainment side. The human side. The side of evoking emotions and hopes. The funny side. The asshole side. The confidence and arrogance of do your job and I'll do mine.

I needed to combine the showman of the first twenty years with the knowledge and strategy of the last ten years to give the hybrid — the sum. Not one or the other. Both. The guy who can follow a DJ at two in the morning and the guy who can design a corporate experience using design thinking. The guy who failed the mask exercise and the guy who can now read a room in ninety seconds. Not a cleaned-up version. The whole thing.

And underneath that identity question was a harder one: does being good at this give me permission to build a platform around it?

I detect weakness quickly. I carry a strong anti-bullshit ethic. I reject manipulation, polish without substance, confidence without integrity. I can feel when something is off before I can explain why — the way that theatre teacher could see through the mask. That sensitivity was built from twenty years of rooms. But sensitivity and authority are not the same thing.

The tension: I know I'm good at holding rooms. I know I can teach this. But there's a gap between being comfortable doing the thing and claiming the right to be the person who teaches the thing. That gap doesn't close with credentials. It closes with deciding that the work you've done — the failures, the rooms, the years — is enough to stand on.

I'm still closing it.

---

The question that a friend asked me — "Anthony, what do you want to say?" — was the turning point. Not because the answer was ready. Because the question forced me to confront the fact that for over a decade, I'd been amplifying other people's voices and never finding my own.

I couldn't stay in the safety of performing anymore.

The earlier version of me — the ultimate performer — valued excellence, execution, polish, confidence, command. Being exceptional within accepted standards. There is nothing wrong with that. It built a deep respect for craft that never went away.

But it had a ceiling. Excellence inside someone else's definition of excellent. Mastery inside inherited rules. Following the game instead of changing how it's played.

The shift was not a rejection of performance. It was the realization that performance alone is an incomplete center. Mastery still matters. Structure still matters. Vocal control still matters. But these are the foundation, not the destination. The destination is authorship.

How do I master the craft deeply enough to build and teach a more relevant, more owned, more modern way?

That's the trailblazer move.

Not rebel without discipline. Not reject fundamentals. Not abandon structure. Not chase novelty for its own sake.

Master the old craft. Understand why the rules existed. See where they still hold. See where they no longer fit. Adapt deliberately. Own a distinct lane grounded in skill.

---

There were moments along the way when other people saw it before I did.

At an event, the director of the show — Fanny — stopped the tech team from putting "MC" under my name on screen. She said no, that's so reductive. He is more than MC.

At another event, a first-time attendee told someone she'd enjoyed "that speaker." The person she was talking to — someone who'd seen me at events for years — was confused. "What speaker? Oh, you mean Anthony." People who saw me for the first time saw a speaker. People who had worked with me for years still called me the MC. The identity was shifting, but the label hadn't caught up.

And Ben Nemtin — a speaker I admired. After an event where he'd heard my closing, I went up to thank him for his presentation. I didn't want to take his time. He said he'd enjoyed my work. I thanked him again and turned to leave, and he said: "See you on the road next time."

He saw me as a peer. Who knows — one day we'll cross paths because we're both in the same field. Or at least he made me feel that. Maybe I'm imagining that's what he said. But I really believe that's the intention he was trying to convey.

At another event, Ty Bennett — a speaker — quoted me. Directly. During his own presentation. It's always a sign of something real when the speaker quotes the MC. It means the line landed hard enough that they remembered it and used it.

A guest at an event once told me: "You walk happy." That's it. You walk happy. She saw something in how I carry myself before I even open my mouth. That's not a technique. That's a state. And maybe it's the thing I've been trying to name this whole time — the thing the mask exercise was pointing at, the thing the clubs taught me, the thing the three rooms broke open. When you're actually in it, people feel it before you say a word.

After a Zoom happy hour with Mike Lake, the group asked me when my next TED talk is. That is motivating. Not because I'm ready. Because other people see the speaker in me before I've fully claimed it myself.

And in Quebec — lots of immigrants, French their second language. I'm bilingual. I understand them. Not just the language gap. The feeling of operating in a world where fluency is the currency of credibility and you're always slightly behind the people who grew up in it. I know what it feels like to compensate with strategy because the words don't come as fast as the thinking. That's not a weakness. That's a specific kind of intelligence that most native speakers will never develop.

---

The shift is not finished. I'm still in it.

I coached with Sylvie Paquette Lussier about moving from ultimate performer to thought leader. We worked on the gap between having the ideas and being able to articulate them — especially in French. My French isn't as fluid as my English. That creates imposter syndrome. In a market where French is the baseline, where French fluency is the currency of credibility, I compensate with strategy and communication design. I know my strength is not in the language itself — it's in what I do with the language. But the insecurity is real. It doesn't go away just because the room claps.

Sylvie pushed me to record myself talking through my ideas out loud — not writing them, speaking them. Practising articulation. Building flow. Because thought leadership isn't just having the insight. It's being able to say it in a way that other people can carry with them.

That work is ongoing. The trailblazer identity is the direction — intellectually clear, not yet fully embodied.

---

But the rooms keep proving it.

At the Boréa Table Ronde in 2026, the audience participation was the highest in the event's history. Not because questions were planted. Because the room felt like a conversation, not a performance.

The client — Chanelle Turgeon Gervais from Boréa Construction — said it plainly: "Tu écoutes, tu ne fais pas juste ton script, mais tu écoutes réellement ce qui se passe sur scène pour être capable après ça de ressortir les éléments. C'est un gros point de différenciateur parmi les différents animateurs qu'on a eus dans les dernières années."

You listen. You don't just do your script. You actually listen to what's happening on stage. That's a big differentiator from the other MCs we've had in the last few years.

At a sales kickoff earlier, a client had already said it more simply: "You helped us transform our event." Not "you did a great job." Not "you were professional." Transformed.

But Chanelle's words were more validating than any applause I've ever received. Because she named the thing I've been building for twenty years: the ability to enter a room and be inside it — not managing it from outside. Not executing a script. Actually being in conversation with what's happening.

Most MCs present at a room. The best ones are in conversation with it. And that's a preparation choice, not a personality trait.

---

That is Speaker Academy. Not a confidence fix. Not a performance upgrade. A complete system — [[COURSE PHILOSOPHY - Speaker Academy - 2026-04-04|Discover, Design, Deliver]] — for people who already have something worth saying and need the tools to make it land.

The speaker is already there. [[COURSE PHILOSOPHY - Speaker Academy - 2026-04-04|The course removes the layers blocking what was always there.]]

The exercises are meant for them to open up and own it. Instead of taking years or months or failing the way I did. It's distilled. It's worked at. The LiveLab lets them fall and stumble to get to a higher state — the same three rooms in one day I had at twenty-five, compressed into a container where the consequences are survivable.

---

But Speaker Academy is not the destination. It's one expression of something larger.

The through-line across twenty years is not the industry. It's the position. Ring announcer for MMA and boxing — not a fighter. MC for MUDGIRL across Canada — not a woman. Host for Skills Canada — not a tradesperson. Emcee for youth conferences at Salon Pepsi — not in education. Beach Club, Miss Quebec, Canada Day, virtual events for banks, product launches, weddings for fifteen years including as a celebrant, DJ companies where I was both selling the events and running them. The room was never mine by subject matter. It was mine by communication.

That is the proof of concept for everything I teach. Every engagement required reading the room before opening my mouth, building trust before asking anything of the audience, and creating the conditions for the moment to land. That's not MC technique. That's communication design — field-tested across industries, crowds, formats, and emotional registers where there was no option to rely on anything else.

The conviction that runs through all of it: [[MY TRUTHS - Anthony Horng - Living Document|communication is design]]. Not performance. Not personality. Not confidence. Design. The work of understanding what another person needs to receive — not just what you want to say — and building the conditions for that to happen.

The MC work is the proof. Speaker Academy is the methodology. And somewhere ahead — the keynote. The stage where both converge into something specific and defensible to say about communication. Something only twenty years of holding rooms as the outsider who was never the expert could produce.

Not a provider delivering a prescription. Not a performer executing a script. Someone who [[EMERGE SYNTHESIS - Practitioner Strategist and the Word That Hasn't Been Written - 2026-04-19|enters the system]] — the room, the moment, the people — and creates the conditions for something to happen that belongs to everyone in it.

The standard MC arrives with a runsheet. Tracks the clock. Executes the sequence. Announces what's next. The event happens and they manage the surface of it. The MC who enters the system moves with the room because they are inside it. They feel the shift before they can name it. They make the adjustment before the room notices one was needed.

That capacity doesn't come from a briefing document. It comes from twenty years of entering rooms where the domain shifts every time and the only constant is the willingness to be inside whatever is happening. Not presenting at the room. Having a conversation with it.

My nephew Sydney calls me OA. When he was younger, saying Uncle Anthony was just too long. As he got older, he tried once to call me Anthony. It felt wrong to both of us. OA isn't a shortcut. It's a role. The name carries the function. On stage, it works the same way. The audience doesn't need to know Anthony. They need to trust the role he holds. When the role is clear, the name becomes secondary. When the role is unclear, even the name feels wrong.

The greatest challenge in communication isn't saying the right words.

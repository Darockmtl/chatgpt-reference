# PSWP Framework Reference

## Purpose

PSWP is a practical narrative structure for MC intros and transitions.

It is built to solve one problem: audiences decide to pay attention based on relevance, not on agenda announcements. PSWP lets an MC create relevance fast, without sounding like a subject matter expert and without overselling.

Use PSWP for:
1. The main conference opening
2. Each session introduction
3. Transitions between sessions
4. Recaps before breaks and re entry moments after breaks

## PSWP in one sentence

Name the **Problem**, signal the **Shift**, explain **Why** it matters now, and land the **Payoff** for the audience.

## What PSWP is and is not

### PSWP is
A framing structure for attention and clarity.
A tool for making sessions feel connected to the audience’s world.
A repeatable format that stays consistent across a full agenda.

### PSWP is not
A storytelling arc like Hero’s Journey.
A teaching model.
A persuasion script for selling a sponsor.
A summary of the session content.

## Core principle

Audience perceived value first.

The MC’s role is to guide attention and create continuity. Authority belongs to the speakers and panelists. PSWP sets them up by making the topic feel necessary.

## The PSWP components

### P: Problem

#### What it is
A single tension the audience recognizes.

#### What it does
Creates instant attention and recognition.
Signals that the introduction is about the audience’s reality, not a corporate statement.

#### What it sounds like
A sharp question.
A concise tension statement.
A contrast between what is happening and what is still not working.

#### Rules
Keep it to one problem.
Avoid exaggeration and absolutes unless the client has approved them.
Avoid invented consequences, stats, or claims.
Avoid teaching language.

#### MC checklist
Can a busy listener say “yes, that’s true” in the first 5 to 10 seconds.
Is the problem specific enough to feel real.
Is it short enough to be delivered cleanly.

#### Common mistakes
Too broad: “Healthcare is changing.”
Too moralizing: “We must do better.”
Too technical too soon.
Overpromising by implying the session will fix the problem.

#### Template lines
“What if the biggest barrier is not X, but Y.”
“Despite progress in X, Y still gets in the way.”
“We are seeing X accelerate, but Y still gets in the way.”

### S: Shift

#### What it is
The direction the conference or session is taking in response to the problem.

This is where the MC names the theme, the session focus, or the official definition when available.

#### What it does
Orients the audience quickly.
Creates a clear mental box for what they are about to hear.
Keeps the intro from becoming a list of topics.

#### What it sounds like
“This session focuses on…”
“Our theme is…”
Official definition, if one exists, delivered as a quote or formal line.

#### Rules
Do not claim outcomes.
Do not add new features that are not in the notes.
Keep it concrete and short.

#### MC checklist
Does the shift answer “what are we doing about the problem” in one sentence.
Is it free of hype words.
Is the definition accurate and approved.

#### Common mistakes
Making the shift a summary of the whole agenda.
Leading with sponsor positioning instead of the topic.
Adding extra claims to make it sound impressive.

#### Template lines
“Our central theme is…”
“This session explores…”
“Connected care is defined as…”

### W: Why

#### What it is
Why the audience should care now.

This can include urgency, context, timing, and relevance to the audience mix. It is the point where virtual audiences decide whether to stay engaged or multitask.

#### What it does
Connects the topic to the audience’s roles and decisions.
Explains why the topic matters in this moment.
Frames the session as timely and necessary.

#### What it sounds like
A short context statement.
A “why now” paragraph.
A role relevant translation: providers, benefits leaders, pharmacy leaders, public sector.

#### Rules
No invented consequences.
No invented data.
No universal claims unless approved.
Keep it grounded in provided notes.

#### MC checklist
Is this tied to the audience, not the speaker.
Does it avoid sounding like a lecture.
Is it short enough to keep momentum.

#### Common mistakes
Using “why” to add new claims.
Turning “why” into a long list.
Making AI or any single tool the headline when the theme is broader.

#### Template lines
“Why now: …”
“For this room, this matters because…”
“In Canada, this lands differently because…”

### P: Payoff

#### What it is
The audience’s benefit from paying attention.

Payoff is not “we will teach you.” Payoff is what the audience will understand more clearly, be able to align on, or be able to act on after listening.

#### What it does
Gives the audience a reason to stay.
Prevents the intro from feeling like an agenda readout.
Signals what to listen for.

#### What it sounds like
A practical value promise that avoids guarantees.
A listening lens: what to listen for, what the session will clarify.
A transition into the next speaker.

#### Rules
Avoid guarantees and absolute outcomes.
Avoid training claims.
Keep it brief.

#### MC checklist
Is the payoff audience centered.
Is it practical.
Is it credible based on the agenda.

#### Common mistakes
Overpromising: “You will leave with a complete roadmap.”
Being vague: “This will be great.”
Restating the session title.

#### Template lines
“By the end of this session, you will have clearer insight into…”
“The value here is understanding what it takes to…”
“Listen for what changes when…”

## How to apply PSWP at different scales

### Conference opening
Problem: system level tension.
Shift: theme and official definition or tagline.
Why: why now and why this theme.
Payoff: what the day will help the audience clarify.

### Session introduction
Problem: one tension specific to that session.
Shift: what the session explores.
Why: relevance to the audience segment.
Payoff: what to listen for and why it is useful.

### Transition between sessions
Problem: one line bridge tension.
Shift: name the next focus.
Why: one relevance line.
Payoff: one listening cue.

## Virtual delivery rules

### Rule 1: Win attention early
The first 15 to 20 seconds decide retention. Lead with Problem as a question or sharp tension.

### Rule 2: Keep sentence load low
Shorter sentences. Fewer nested clauses. Pause friendly phrasing.

### Rule 3: Reduce agenda language
“We will hear from…” is fine, but it should not be the dominant middle of the intro. Add a payoff line so it does not sound like a schedule announcement.

### Rule 4: Avoid sponsor tone
If sponsor positioning is required, place it late and frame it as convening rationale. Keep it short.

## Variation rule to avoid templated intros

Keep PSWP constant, vary the opening move.

Approved opening moves:
1. Problem opening
2. Consequence opening, only if the consequence is in the provided notes
3. Question opening, based on the Problem

After each draft, choose the opening move you want next.

## Quick build templates

### Template A: Conference opening
Problem: [One sentence tension]
Shift: [Theme + official definition or tagline]
Why: [Why now, based on approved notes]
Payoff: [What the audience will gain across the day]

### Template B: Session intro
Problem: [Tension tied to the session]
Shift: [What the session explores]
Why: [Why it matters for this audience segment]
Payoff: [What to listen for]

### Template C: Transition line
Problem: [Bridge tension]
Shift: [Next session focus]
Why: [One relevance sentence]
Payoff: [One listening cue]

## Quality control checklist

Before finalizing any intro:
1. No new facts added.
2. No invented consequences.
3. One problem only.
4. Shift is clear and not promotional.
5. Why is audience relevant and short.
6. Payoff is practical and credible.
7. Spoken flow: sentences are speakable and breath friendly.
8. Sponsor positioning, if present, is late and minimal.

## Common language traps to avoid

1. Teaching claims
Avoid: “We will teach you how to…”
Prefer: “We will examine what it takes to…”

2. Absolutes
Avoid: “This is the only way…”
Prefer: “This is a major barrier…” if approved.

3. Moralizing
Avoid: “We must…”
Prefer: “The focus today is…”

4. Overhype
Avoid: “Revolutionary, game changing…”
Prefer: concrete nouns and verbs.

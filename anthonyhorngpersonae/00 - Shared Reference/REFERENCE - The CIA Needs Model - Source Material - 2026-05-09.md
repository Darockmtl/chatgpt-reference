# REFERENCE — The CIA Needs Model — Source Material

**Source:** Changingminds.org — https://changingminds.org/explanations/needs/cia.htm
*Raw clipping:** [[The CIA Needs Model]]
**Author:** Site is run by Changing Works (David Straker). The CIA model is the site's own synthesis of older needs frameworks.
**Status of model:** Practitioner synthesis, not peer-reviewed research. The component needs are well-grounded in academic literature; the specific three-part packaging into CIA is one practitioner's consolidation.

This file documents the model as the source describes it. Speaker Academy's application and extension of the model is in [[EXTRACT - CIA as Guiding Lens - Teaching Unit - 2026-05-09]].

---

## What CIA Is

CIA stands for **Control, Identity, and Arousal** — three needs the source proposes as the central drivers of how people receive persuasion. The needs are listed in the priority order in which the source claims they are experienced.

The source positions CIA as an update of an earlier "CIN" model in which the third need was Novelty rather than Arousal. The change reflects the source's view that Arousal is a deeper and more encompassing need than Novelty alone.

---

## The Three Needs

### Control

The need for the sense of being safe and able to bend the environment to one's purposes.

The source ties Control to evolutionary survival — an organism that can predict and influence its environment can stay healthy long enough to procreate. Control is positioned as the deepest of the three needs because it sits closest to physical survival.

Control covers what Maslow placed in the lower hierarchy (health, safety) but extends into cognitive and social control as well — the ability to anticipate, understand, and act effectively.

### Identity

The need to know who you are, especially relative to other people.

The source ties Identity to social survival. It maps to Maslow's higher needs — belonging, esteem, self-actualization. The source's framing: Identity is the architecture by which a person locates themselves in their social world.

### Arousal

The need to know that one is learning, improving, evolving — and the need to compete.

The source defines Arousal broadly. It includes curiosity, challenge, novelty, and stimulation. Arousal is positioned as the "highest" need in the hierarchy — it activates only when the lower needs (Control, Identity) are sufficiently stable.

---

## The Priority Order

The source claims the order is **Control → Identity → Arousal** by depth. Deeper needs override higher needs when threatened.

The reasoning in the source:
- Control is about staying alive (survival).
- Identity is about social belonging (social survival).
- Arousal is about thriving rather than merely surviving.

A threatened Control system overrides Identity and Arousal needs. A threatened Identity overrides Arousal. The order is presented as a structural fact about how the nervous system protects itself.

---

## The Most Operationally Important Claim

> "We do not always *need* control and identity — what is important in most cases is that we *believe* that we have them. Hence the need is for a *sense* of them."

This is the source's central operational insight. People don't need to actually have control or identity — they need to believe they have them. The brain accepts a believable model in place of ground truth.

This applies to all three needs but is named explicitly for Control and Identity. The implication for persuasion: producing the *felt experience* of a need being met can substitute for actually meeting it.

## Underpinning Sub-Needs

The source names specific sub-needs that build each of the three.

### For Control:
- **Completion, certainty, winning** — signals that one is "getting there"
- **Understanding, consistency** — help us predict (and hence control) what will happen
- **Repetition** — confirms our beliefs and models as still valid
- **Prediction** — the operational output of Control

### For Identity:
- **Belonging** — to a group lends us the identity of the group
- **Conformity** — to the group's rules to stay inside
- **Esteem of others** — raises sense of identity, affords status
- **Status** — relative position in a hierarchy
- **Winning** — everyone likes a winner, so we try to succeed (overlaps with Control)
- **Explaining** — being able to explain casts us as expert and rational
- **Rationality** — being seen as someone who thinks clearly

### For Arousal:
- **Curiosity** — kicks us into action when boredom hits
- **Challenge** — achievable challenges stimulate us
- **Novelty** — attracts us
- **Anticipation** — of pleasurable or painful arousal attracts or repels us. The source notes: "pain can still be better than no arousal."

## Inner and Outer

The source distinguishes inner and outer aspects of each need. The table is presented as indicative, not definitive.

| | Inner | Outer |
|---|---|---|
| **Control** | Health, self-control, inner calm, confidence, knowledge and skills | Safety, control of other people, trust in other people |
| **Identity** | Clear beliefs and values, integrity, self-knowledge | Connecting with others, belonging to groups, esteem of others, social status |
| **Arousal** | Imagination and creativity, problem-solving, day-dreaming | Social interaction, physical contact with others, new experiences |

---

## The Structural Tension

The source names one specific dynamic: **Control and Arousal pull against each other.**

> "Our need for arousal and control can conflict. Control is about stability. Arousal may lead to change. As control is the deeper need, it often displaces arousal. So if a competitor is seeking arousal, you could shake their sense of control."

This is the only interaction between the three needs that the source explicitly names. Other interactions (Identity/Control, Identity/Arousal) are not specified.

---

## Related Systems

The source maps CIA to several earlier frameworks:

| CIA model | Control | Identity | Arousal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **James (1892)** | Material | Social | Spiritual |
| **Alderfer (1972)** | Existence | Relatedness | Growth |
| **Mathes (1981)** | Physiological | Belongingness | Self-actualization |
| **Deci/Ryan (1991)** | Autonomy | Relatedness | Competence |

The source also notes that Maslow's hierarchy maps onto CIA — the lower Maslow needs (health, safety) sit in Control, the higher needs (belonging, esteem, actualization) sit in Identity.

The source claims CIA has "proven particularly useful in situations of practical persuasion" — its positioning is operational rather than purely theoretical.

---

## The Source's Persuasion Application

The source frames CIA as a tool for influence:

> "Build tension by impacting their needs, possibly through the systems they have already built to satisfy those needs. Create closure by offering solutions that meet needs."

> "Give control through such as choice or involvement. Boost identity through such as inclusion or admiration. Offer arousal through such as novelty or challenge."

The model is built for active persuasion — threatening needs to create movement, satisfying needs to create closure. This is worth noting: CIA's source orientation is *engineered influence*, not just diagnostic reading. Speaker Academy's application of CIA reframes it toward reading rather than engineering, but the source itself leans toward influence.
